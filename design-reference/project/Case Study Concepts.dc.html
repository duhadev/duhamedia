Work/Study 01

The demand was already there. The store was leaking it.

Northside Goods had three stores, healthy search traffic and a checkout that lost roughly two of every three carts. Fixing acquisition first would have cost them $40K to learn nothing. Here is the order we worked in, and why.

ClientNorthside Goods
SectorHardgoods retail, 3 locations
EngagementBuild + engine, 12 months
PublishedFeb 2026, client-approved

01 — What we found

Two weeks of audit, no changes shipped. Sessions were up 31% year over year while revenue was flat, which usually means the leak is after the click. It was: a theme carrying four legacy apps, 2.4s of blocking script before first paint, and a five-field checkout on a store where 68% of sessions were mobile.

The second finding mattered more. Their conversion tracking double-counted returning customers, so every channel report they had been buying decisions on was wrong by roughly 40%. Nobody was lying to them. The numbers were just built badly.

02 — The order of work

Tracking first, because every later decision depends on it. Then the theme rebuild, because paid traffic into a leaking store buys more leaks. Only in month four did we touch acquisition, and by then a 2% lift in checkout completion was worth more than any bid change we could have made in month one.

Scaling spend into a store that leaks demand just buys more leaks.

The tempting alternative — restructure the ad account in week one, show a fast ROAS win — would have worked on paper for about six weeks. It fails because the win is measured on the same broken tracking that caused the problem, and because the checkout ceiling doesn't move.

03 — What changed, month by month

Weeks 1–2 audit. Weeks 3–4 tracking rebuild and a clean baseline. Months 2–3 theme rebuild, checkout cut to two fields, 900ms off first paint. Month 4 email lifecycle. Months 5–8 paid, scaled against the new baseline. Month 9 handover: their marketing manager took the calendar, the ad account and the playbook.

04 — What we'd do differently

We should have started the handover training in month five, not month eight. Their manager was ready earlier than our plan assumed, and two months of shadowing would have been enough.

Sessions up 31%. Revenue flat. The leak was after the click.

Two weeks of audit before a single change shipped, then nine months of work in a deliberate order. The rail on the right holds every number in this story so you can check the argument as you read it.

01 — Diagnosis

Traffic growth with flat revenue is a conversion problem or a measurement problem. Here it was both. The theme carried four legacy apps and 2.4s of blocking script; checkout asked for five fields on a store where 68% of sessions were mobile.

Worse, conversion tracking double-counted returning customers. Every channel report the owner had been making decisions on overstated paid performance by about 40%.

02 — Sequence, and the case against the shortcut

Tracking, then theme, then acquisition. The shortcut — restructure ads in week one for a fast ROAS headline — measures the win on the same broken data and leaves the checkout ceiling in place. It looks good for about six weeks.

By month four a 2-point gain in checkout completion was worth more than any bid strategy available in month one. That is the whole argument for the order, and it is checkable against the rail.

03 — Handover

From month six their marketing manager ran the weekly review with us in the room. Month nine she ran it without us: calendar, ad account, playbook, reporting. Fees went to zero on schedule.

In hindsight the training should have started in month five. She was ready before our plan assumed she would be.
